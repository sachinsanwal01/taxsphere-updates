MOA & AOA Compliance Guide

What Sections 4, 5, 6, 10 and 13–18 of the Companies Act, 2013 require of a Memorandum and Articles of Association, how each clause is altered, and what a default costs — read this before relying on the draft in the Generator tab.

Scope note: This guide summarises the standing law under Chapter II (Sections 3–22) of the Companies Act, 2013, the Companies (Incorporation) Rules, 2014, and related provisions cited against each item. Monetary penalties shown reflect the Companies (Amendment) Act, 2020 re-categorisation of most procedural defaults from "fine/imprisonment" to civil "penalty", effective 21 December 2020. Always verify the current position on mca.gov.in before relying on any figure below.

1. MOA & AOA in a nutshell

The Memorandum of Association (MOA) is the company's charter — it defines the company's name, home State, the objects it may pursue, the extent of members' liability, its capital structure and the subscribers who bring it into existence (Section 4). The Articles of Association (AOA) are the company's internal rule-book — they regulate how the company is actually run: share transfers, general meetings, the Board, dividends, accounts and winding up (Section 5). The relationship between the two is hierarchical: the AOA is always subordinate to the MOA, and both are subordinate to the Act itself. Section 6 renders void any provision in the MOA or AOA that is inconsistent with the Act, and any AOA provision that conflicts with the MOA is equally ineffective to that extent.

Rule of thumb used throughout this guide: the MOA tells the world what the company can do and how far members are exposed; the AOA tells insiders how the company is actually managed day to day.

Section 10 gives both documents contractual force: once registered, the MOA and AOA bind the company and every member as if each member had separately signed and sealed them, agreeing to be bound by every provision. This creates three overlapping "contracts" recognised by company law — between the company and each member, between the members inter se (though enforced through the company), and between the company and its officers in their capacity as such (though this last strand is narrower and does not import every AOA right into an officer's individual service contract).

2. Contents of the Memorandum (Section 4)

ClauseWhat it must stateKey sub-section
I. NameThe company's name, ending in "Limited" (public) or "Private Limited" (private), with the OPC and Section 8 variations noted above. The proposed name must not be identical or too similar to an existing company/LLP/registered trademark, and must not fall foul of the Emblems and Names (Prevention of Improper Use) Act, 1950 or Rule 8/8A/8B undesirable-name criteria.Sec. 4(1)(a), (2)–(3)
II. Registered officeOnly the State (and Union Territory, where relevant) in which the registered office is situated — the full address is intimated separately to the Registrar within 30 days of incorporation (Section 12), not stated in the MOA itself.Sec. 4(1)(b)
III. ObjectsThe objects for which the company is incorporated, and matters considered necessary in furtherance of them. Since the Companies (Amendment) Act, 2015 removed the mandatory "objects clause" split into main/ancillary/other objects, a single unified objects statement is legally sufficient, though many drafters still separate the two for clarity.Sec. 4(1)(c)
IV. LiabilityWhether members' liability is limited by shares, limited by guarantee (with the guaranteed amount), or unlimited.Sec. 4(1)(d)
V. CapitalFor a company having share capital: the authorised share capital, its division into shares of a fixed amount, and the number of shares each subscriber takes (at least one share each).Sec. 4(1)(e)
VI. Association / SubscriptionA declaration that the subscribers wish to form a company and agree to take the shares opposite their names, followed by each subscriber's name, address, description, occupation and signature, attested by one witness.Sec. 4(1)(e)(iii), Sec. 7

A private company's MOA/AOA must additionally restrict the right to transfer shares, cap membership at 200 (excluding present/former employee-members), and prohibit any invitation to the public to subscribe for securities — these are the three defining marks of "private company" under Section 2(68), and dropping any one of them (by altering the Articles) converts the company into a public company by operation of Section 14(1) even without a name change.

3. Contents of the Articles (Section 5)

Section 5(6) prescribes model-article forms in Schedule I: Table F (company limited by shares), Table G (guarantee company having share capital, which imports Table F), Table H (guarantee company without share capital), Table I (unlimited company having share capital, which imports Table F) and Table J (unlimited company without share capital). A company may adopt all, some, or none of the applicable Table's regulations (Section 5(7)); for a company registered after the Act commenced, any regulation it has not expressly excluded or modified continues to apply automatically by default (Section 5(8)). Table F itself runs to a full set of numbered regulations covering share capital and variation of class rights, share certificates, lien, calls, transfer and transmission, forfeiture, alteration of capital, capitalisation of profits, buy-back, general meetings and proceedings, voting rights and proxies, the Board of Directors and its proceedings, borrowing powers, dividends and reserves, accounts and audit, the common seal (now optional — Section 22 was amended in 2015 to make a seal non-mandatory), winding up, and officer indemnity.

Articles may also contain an entrenchment provision under Section 5(3)–(4): specified provisions may be made alterable only by a more restrictive procedure or condition than an ordinary special resolution (e.g. unanimity, or a higher voting threshold) — provided the entrenching provision is itself only inserted by all members' agreement (private company) or by a special resolution (public company). See Section 10 of this guide.

4. Altering the Name clause

A name change follows Section 13(2)–(3) read with Section 4(2)–(3): the company reserves the new name (via the RUN service or as part of another integrated form), then passes a special resolution, and the change takes effect only when the Central Government (delegated to the Registrar) approves it and issues a fresh Certificate of Incorporation bearing the new name. No such approval is required where the only change is adding or deleting the word "Private" upon conversion between company classes (Section 13(2) proviso) — a simple special resolution and MGT-14 suffice there.

5. Altering the Registered Office clause

Because Clause II states only the State, only a move that crosses a State (or a Registrar's local jurisdiction) counts as altering the Memorandum under Section 13; a move within the same city/town/village is a Board-level, non-MOA change under Section 12(4)–(5) alone. Section 13(5) bars an interstate shift while any inquiry, inspection, investigation or prosecution is pending against the company, subject to limited exceptions under approved insolvency resolution plans.

6. Altering the Objects clause

Objects are altered like any other Memorandum clause — special resolution, MGT-14 within 30 days — but Section 13(9) additionally obliges the Registrar to register the alteration and issue a certificate within 30 days of the special resolution being filed. Where the company has raised money from the public through a prospectus and any part remains unutilised, Section 13(8) imposes extra conditions before the special resolution can even be passed: the resolution must be preceded by a published advertisement and individual communication to shareholders disclosing the justification for the change, the notice must be filed with SEBI (for listed companies) and other prescribed particulars, and dissenting shareholders must be given an exit opportunity by promoters/controlling shareholders in the manner prescribed by SEBI regulations.

Rule 32 of the Companies (Incorporation) Rules, 2014 sets out the detailed advertisement and dissent-exit mechanics for a Section 13(8) objects change.

7. Altering the Liability clause

Section 13(11) voids any alteration of a guarantee company's memorandum that purports to give a person a right to participate in divisible profits otherwise than as a member. More fundamentally, Section 18 lets an existing company convert from one class to another (e.g. unlimited company re-registering as limited, or a private company converting to a Section 8-style structure) by altering both the Memorandum and Articles in accordance with Chapter II, but such a conversion cannot affect debts, liabilities, obligations or contracts incurred before conversion — those survive unchanged (Section 18(3)). Increasing a member's liability, or requiring a member to subscribe for more shares, is only binding on that member with their prior written consent (Section 13(11)/Section 38 general principle) — a company cannot force greater exposure onto an existing member by ordinary majority alteration alone.

8. Altering the Capital clause

Section 61 lists the ways a limited company having share capital may alter it if its Articles authorise doing so: increasing authorised capital, consolidating and dividing shares into larger-denomination shares, converting fully paid shares into stock (and reconverting), sub-dividing shares into smaller-denomination shares, and cancelling unissued shares (not a reduction of capital). None of these needs Tribunal approval — an ordinary resolution in a general meeting is generally sufficient unless the Articles require more — but the company must still notify the Registrar.

9. Altering the Articles & converting company class (Section 14)

A company may alter its Articles by special resolution, including alterations that have the effect of converting a private company into a public company or vice versa. Two asymmetric safeguards apply:

  • Private → Public: once the altered Articles drop the Section 2(68) private-company restrictions, the company becomes a public company from the date of alteration — no Central Government or Tribunal approval is required for the conversion itself. File MGT-14 within 30 days; if the name also drops "Private" consequentially, no separate CG approval is needed for that name change either (Section 13(2) proviso), though a fresh COI is still issued.
  • Public → Private: the conversion is not valid unless approved by an order of the Tribunal (NCLT), which may impose conditions. Once the order is obtained, file Form INC-27 with the Registrar, together with a printed copy of the altered Articles, within 15 days of receiving the Tribunal's order (Rule 33, Companies (Incorporation) Rules, 2014).

An Article cannot lawfully contain anything that conflicts with the Memorandum or the Act (Section 6); such a provision is void to the extent of the inconsistency even if registered without objection.

10. Entrenchment provisions

Section 5(3) allows the Articles to entrench specified provisions so that they can only be altered by a procedure or condition more restrictive than a special resolution — commonly unanimity, or a supermajority higher than 75%. Entrenchment may be inserted only at incorporation, or later by a resolution agreed to by all members (private company) or by special resolution (public company) — Section 5(4). Notice must reach the Registrar: at incorporation, via Form INC-2 (OPC) or INC-7; for an existing company, via Form MGT-14 within 30 days of the date the entrenchment takes effect. Entrenchment is a useful minority-protection device in joint-venture and investor-shareholder agreements, since it prevents a bare special-resolution majority from later stripping out negotiated protections.

11. Underlying doctrines

Doctrine of ultra vires

An act outside the objects stated in the Memorandum is ultra vires the company — void from the outset, incapable of ratification even by a unanimous vote of every member, though the company itself cannot always escape restitution obligations that flow from an ultra vires transaction. In practice, the 2015 shift to a unified objects clause and the routine inclusion of broad "matters necessary in furtherance" language has narrowed the doctrine's bite, but it has not been abolished — a transaction with no rational connection to any stated object remains exposed.

Doctrine of constructive notice

Because the MOA and AOA are public documents on the MCA registry once filed, every person dealing with the company is deemed to have notice of their contents — a third party cannot later claim ignorance of a restriction that was there to be found (e.g. a cap on a director's borrowing authority stated in the Articles).

Doctrine of indoor management (the "Turquand rule")

As a counterweight, an outsider dealing with the company in good faith is entitled to assume that the company's own internal procedural requirements (board approvals, quorum, internal authorisations) have been properly complied with, even if they in fact were not — the outsider is not expected to audit the company's internal minute-books. This protection has recognised limits: it does not help an outsider who had actual notice of the irregularity, who behaved with suspicion or negligence, who relied on a forged document, or where the act was ultra vires the company altogether (as opposed to merely an internal procedural lapse) or ultra vires the officer's authority in a way apparent on the face of the public documents.

12. Penalties ready-reckoner

Most MOA/AOA procedural defaults were re-cast by the Companies (Amendment) Act, 2020 as civil "penalties" imposed departmentally (via adjudicating officers) rather than court-prosecuted fines — faster to levy, but still enforceable as arrears. Section 446B gives a 50% reduction (subject to the stated caps) to One Person Companies, small companies, start-ups, producer companies and Section 8 companies for most such penalties.
