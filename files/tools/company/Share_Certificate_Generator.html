2. Form PAS-3: Return of Allotment

Form PAS-3 is the return every company files with the Registrar to record that it has allotted securities — equity or preference shares, debentures, or any other security — and to whom. It is distinct from the share certificate: PAS-3 is the company's filing to the ROC; SH-1 is the certificate the company issues to the allottee. Both flow from the same allotment, on different clocks.

3. PAS-3 Due Dates

Until PAS-3 is actually filed for a private placement, the company cannot use the money it raised — Section 42(4) blocks utilisation of private-placement proceeds pending the return, even though the shares have already been allotted.

4. PAS-3 Attachments & Certification

  • List of allottees — name, address, PAN, and the number and class of securities allotted to each.
  • The Board (and, where required, shareholders') resolution approving the allotment, and its SRN from Form MGT-14 if separately filed.
  • Valuation report, where the consideration was other than cash.
  • For private placement: reference to the PAS-4 private placement offer letter and the special resolution under Section 42.
  • Certification by a practising Company Secretary, Chartered Accountant or Cost Accountant — mandatory except for a One Person Company or a small company.

Share Certificate & Transfer Compliance Guide

What Sections 46 & 56 and the Companies (Share Capital and Debentures) Rules, 2014 require when a company issues a share certificate (Form SH-1), registers a transfer (Form SH-4), or notifies a transferee of partly-paid securities (Form SH-5) — read this before relying on the drafts in the other tabs.

This guide is general awareness material, not legal advice. Timelines, penalty amounts and signing rules below reflect the Companies Act, 2013 as commonly summarised by professional sources; always check the current text of the Act, the Rules and your company's Articles before acting.

1. What Form SH-1 Is

Form No. SH-1 is the format prescribed by Rule 5(2) of the Companies (Share Capital and Debentures) Rules, 2014 for a certificate of shares issued under Section 46 of the Companies Act, 2013. A share certificate is prima facie evidence of a member's title to the shares named in it, and it is issued under the common seal, if any, of the company (or otherwise as authorised by its Articles) specifying the shares held, their distinctive numbers (for physical shares) and the amount paid-up.

3. Who Must Sign

Under Rule 5(3)/(4), a share certificate must be signed by two directors, or by one director and the company secretary where the company has appointed one. In a One Person Company, a single director's signature suffices, together with the company secretary if one is appointed. The signatures are usually facsimile or manual, per the Articles, and the certificate is issued under the common seal, if the company has one.

4. Common Seal

Since the Companies (Amendment) Act, 2015, a common seal is optional. Where a company has a common seal, it should be affixed to the certificate in the presence of the signing directors/officer. Where a company has resolved not to have a common seal, the certificate is validly issued without one, so long as it is signed as the Articles require.

5. Distinctive Numbers & Dematerialised Shares

Distinctive numbers identify each physical share and must appear on a certificate for shares held in physical form. Shares held in dematerialised form through a depository do not carry distinctive numbers or a physical certificate at all — the depository's records, not a Form SH-1, are the evidence of title. Use this generator only for physical share certificates.

6. Register of Members

The particulars of every share certificate issued — folio, distinctive numbers, class, amount paid-up and the holder's details — must be entered in the Register of Members under Section 88 at or about the same time the certificate is issued, and the certificate should be issued pursuant to a resolution of the Board (or a committee authorised by the Board).

7. Duplicate Certificates

Rule 6 governs duplicate certificates issued in place of one that is proved to have been lost, destroyed, defaced, mutilated or torn. The usual requirements are Board approval, a letter of indemnity (with surety where the paid-up value exceeds the prescribed threshold), an affidavit in cases of loss or destruction, applicable fees, and an entry in the Register of Renewed and Duplicate Share Certificates maintained under Rule 6(3)(b). Every duplicate certificate must be marked "Duplicate issued in lieu of Certificate No. ___". Issuing a duplicate certificate with intent to defraud is an offence under Section 46(5), attracting fine on the company and action under Section 447 against every officer in default — check the current quantum before advising, as penalty figures have been revised by amendment.

8. Stamp Duty

Stamp duty on the issue of a share certificate (payable by the company on issue, typically under the state Stamp Act or the Indian Stamp Act as applicable) is distinct from the stamp duty payable on a subsequent transfer of shares (traditionally borne by the transferee/transferor on the transfer instrument). Rates vary by state and have also been affected by the centralised stamp duty regime introduced from 1 July 2020 for listed/dematerialised securities — verify the applicable rate before relying on this generator for stamping purposes.

9. Penalty for Default

Where a company fails to issue certificates within the timelines in Section 56(4), it and every officer in default is liable to the penalty prescribed under Section 56(6), in addition to any liability under Section 447 where fraud is involved. Professional sources commonly cite a company-level fine in the range of roughly Rs. 25,000 to Rs. 5,00,000 and an officer-level fine in the range of roughly Rs. 10,000 to Rs. 1,00,000, but always confirm the current figures (these provisions have been amended over time) before advising a client.

10. Overview & Concept of Transfer of Securities

"Transfer of securities" is the voluntary act of a member conveying title in their shares, debentures or other securities to another person — by sale, gift, settlement or exchange — by delivering a duly executed instrument of transfer. It is deliberately distinct from transmission, which vests title by operation of law (on death, succession, inheritance, insolvency or lunacy of the holder) without any instrument of transfer, and which the company recognises on production of proof such as a succession certificate, probate or letters of administration rather than a Form SH-4. Both are dealt with together under Section 56 of the Companies Act, 2013, but only a transfer needs this generator.

Section 44 makes the shares, debentures and other interests of any member movable property, transferable in the manner provided by the company's Articles — the starting legal proposition that lets securities change hands at all. Section 56 then lays down the mechanics: the company may not register a transfer of, or the interest of a member in, securities unless a proper instrument of transfer (Form SH-4) in the prescribed form has been delivered to it, duly stamped, dated and executed, within the prescribed time and accompanied by the certificate (or letter of allotment). "Securities" for this purpose covers equity shares, preference shares and debentures, and other instruments as defined in Section 2(81) read with Section 2(h) of the Securities Contracts (Regulation) Act, 1956.

Physical and dematerialised securities move differently. A physical transfer is effected only through Form SH-4 lodged with the company; this generator produces that form. A dematerialised transfer takes place off the company's books entirely — the transferor instructs their depository participant (via a delivery instruction slip or its electronic equivalent) and the depository records the change of beneficial ownership under the Depositories Act, 1996, and applicable SEBI (Depositories and Participants) Regulations; no Form SH-4, no stamped instrument delivered to the company, and no company-level 60-day clock apply to that leg of the transaction.

Transferability is not unlimited. Under Section 58(2), the securities of a public company are freely transferable, though contracts or arrangements between members (e.g. a shareholders' agreement) remain enforceable as ordinary contracts. Private companies almost always restrict transfer through their Articles — commonly a right of first refusal or pre-emption right in favour of existing members, and a Board power to decline registration — consistent with the definition of a private company under Section 2(68). Listed companies layer on SEBI (LODR) Regulations, depository rules and any lock-in conditions from the offer document. Under Section 58(1)/(4), a Board that refuses to register a transfer must communicate its reasons within thirty days, and the aggrieved transferor or transferee may appeal to the National Company Law Tribunal, which can direct registration, or rectification of the Register of Members under Section 59, on being satisfied the refusal was without sufficient cause.

11. Form SH-4: What It Is & When It's Used

Form No. SH-4 is the "Securities Transfer Form" prescribed by Rule 11(1) of the Companies (Share Capital and Debentures) Rules, 2014 for a transfer of shares (or debentures) under Section 56(1) of the Companies Act, 2013. It is the instrument of transfer that must accompany every physical share transfer — the company cannot register a transfer of physical shares without a duly stamped, dated and executed Form SH-4, except where the law specifically dispenses with it (e.g. transmission by operation of law, or transfers of dematerialised securities effected through a depository, which bypass SH-4 entirely).

13. FEMA Declaration (Transfers Involving a Non-Resident)

Where a transferee is a person resident outside India (or a transferor is), the transferee must confirm whether prior approval under the Foreign Exchange Management (Non-Debt Instruments) Rules, 2019 is required for the transfer, and if so, that it has been obtained and is enclosed. Most resident-to-resident transfers will simply confirm that no such approval is required; cross-border transfers should be checked against the current FEMA/RBI framework (and sectoral caps, where applicable) before the form is executed.

14. Board Approval & the Register of Members

Once a duly executed and stamped SH-4 is lodged (for a private company, always; for other companies, unless dematerialised) the Board (or a delegate under Section 179(3)) approves the transfer, the old holder's entry in the Register of Members is updated and a new/updated folio is opened for the transferee, and the company issues a fresh certificate (or endorses the existing one) to the transferee — within one month of receipt of the instrument of transfer, per Section 56(4)(c).

15. Form SH-5: Notice for Transfer of Partly Paid Securities

Not every share is fully paid on issue — a company can call up the price of a share in instalments, leaving a balance the holder must pay when later "called" by the Board. Form No. SH-5, prescribed by Rule 11(3) of the Companies (Share Capital and Debentures) Rules, 2014 under Section 56(3) of the Act, exists for exactly this situation: whenever a transferor lodges a transfer application alone for securities that are only partly paid, the company cannot register that transfer until it has first sent this notice to the transferee, spelling out precisely how much remains called-but-unpaid and not-yet-called on the securities they are about to receive.

The transferee then has two weeks from receipt of the notice to raise an objection. Silence is treated as consent — if no objection reaches the company within that window, the company proceeds to register the transfer in the ordinary course, and the transferee becomes bound to pay the outstanding calls as and when the Board makes them. If the transferee does object within the two weeks, the company holds the transfer pending resolution of that objection rather than registering it. This mechanism exists purely to protect the transferee from unknowingly inheriting a call liability — it has no equivalent when both transferor and transferee jointly execute the transfer application, since in that case the transferee's signature on the SH-4 already signifies informed acceptance of the partly-paid status.
