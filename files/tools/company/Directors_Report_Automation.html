Director's Report Compliance Guide

What Section 134 and the Companies (Accounts) Rules, 2014 require in a Board's/Director's Report, where each annexure and policy fits, when the approving resolution must additionally be filed in Form MGT-14, and what the Registrar can penalise if any of this is got wrong — read this before relying on the draft in the Generator tab.

Scope note: This guide summarises the standing law under Section 134 of the Companies Act, 2013, the Companies (Accounts) Rules, 2014, Section 117 (Form MGT-14), Section 454 and the Companies (Adjudication of Penalties) Rules, 2014, and related provisions cited against each item below. Thresholds, fees and penalty amounts are amended from time to time, and the illustrative case studies in Section 13 are summarised from published Registrar of Companies adjudication orders for awareness only — always verify the current position on mca.gov.in (including the adjudication-order archive) or with a practising Company Secretary before relying on any figure below.

1. What the Director's Report Must Contain

Section 134(3) of the Companies Act, 2013, read with Rule 8 of the Companies (Accounts) Rules, 2014, requires the Board's Report to be attached to the financial statements laid before the members, and to state a defined list of matters — from the extract of the annual return and the number of Board meetings, to the Directors' Responsibility Statement, dividend, reserves, state of affairs, related-party transactions, loans/investments, CSR, risk management, and conservation of energy. Rule 8(5) adds a further list of disclosures (financial highlights, change in the nature of business, deposits, internal financial controls, significant orders, and more). This tool builds the narrative sections of the report from your financial and governance inputs, and lets you tick every annexure-type disclosure that applies to your company.

The report must be signed on behalf of the Board by the Chairperson (if authorised), or by at least two Directors (one of whom must be the Managing Director, if there is one), or by the sole Director of an OPC, under Section 134(6). Since the Companies (Amendment) Act, 2020, Section 134(8) is a fixed, non-discretionary penalty rather than a range: a company in default under any part of Section 134 is liable to a flat penalty of Rs. 3,00,000, and every officer in default to a flat Rs. 50,000 — imposed by the Registrar of Companies as Adjudicating Officer under Section 454, without the Board needing to prove intent. Small Companies, OPCs, Start-ups and Producer Companies can receive a reduced penalty under Section 446B. See “13. Punishment for Non-Compliance” below for real adjudication orders passed under this very sub-section.

2. Directors' Responsibility Statement

Section 134(5) requires the Board to state that applicable accounting standards were followed, that accounting policies were consistently applied and judgments made were reasonable and prudent, that proper accounting records were maintained, and that annual accounts were prepared on a going concern basis. A fifth clause — that internal financial controls were laid down and operated effectively — applies only where the company is listed. A closing clause on compliance systems for all applicable laws applies to every company. The generator adjusts the clause list automatically based on the "Types of Company" you select.

3. Related Party Transactions & Loans/Investments

Every contract or arrangement with a related party that is not on an arm's length basis or not in the ordinary course of business must be disclosed in Form AOC-2 under Section 188(1), read with Rule 8(2) of the Companies (Accounts) Rules, 2014 — even a "NIL" AOC-2 is conventionally annexed to confirm that no such transaction arose. The prescribed form has two parts: Part 1 covers contracts not at arm's length (which additionally need the particulars of Board approval, and a special resolution under the first proviso to Section 188(1) once the transaction crosses the prescribed value thresholds), and Part 2 covers material contracts that, though at arm's length, the Board has separately approved under the third proviso to Section 188(1). Ticking this item in the Generator tab now drafts the full two-part pro-forma — including a NIL return for either or both parts — as its own lettered Annexure, rather than a placeholder reference. Particulars of loans given, guarantees provided, securities furnished, or investments made under Section 186 must also be disclosed if applicable. The Related Party Transaction Policy (in the Company Policies tab) sets out how the Audit Committee and Board approve such transactions before they are ever entered into.

4. Corporate Social Responsibility (Section 135)

Trigger (any one, in the immediately preceding FY)Threshold
Net worthRs. 500 crore or more
TurnoverRs. 1,000 crore or more
Net profitRs. 5 crore or more

A company crossing any one threshold must spend at least 2% of its average net profit (computed under Section 198) of the three immediately preceding financial years on CSR, through a CSR Committee, in accordance with an Annual Action Plan. Unspent amounts for an ongoing project must move to a separate "Unspent CSR Account" within 30 days of the year-end and be utilised within 3 financial years, failing which the balance goes to a Schedule VII fund; any other unspent amount goes to such a fund within 6 months of the year-end (Section 135(5)/(6)). Projects of Rs. 1 crore or more may additionally require an independent impact assessment under Rule 8(3) of the CSR Rules.

The Companies (Amendment) Bill, 2025 proposes to lower these thresholds (net worth to Rs. 100 crore, turnover to Rs. 500 crore, net profit to Rs. 3 crore) and to require a CSR-experienced director on the Committee. As of the last review of this guide, the Bill had not been notified into force — verify its status before applying the lower figures.

6. Particulars of Employees (Rule 5)

Under Rule 5(2) of the Companies (Appointment and Remuneration of Managerial Personnel) Rules, 2014, the Board's Report must separately name any employee who, throughout the year, received aggregate remuneration of not less than Rs. 1,02,00,000 per annum, or who, for part of the year, received remuneration at a rate of not less than Rs. 8,50,000 per month, as well as any employee drawing more remuneration than the Managing Director/Whole-time Director and holding 2% or more of the equity shares. Regardless of these thresholds, the names of the top 10 employees by remuneration must be disclosed. Rule 5(1) imposes further disclosures (median remuneration ratio, percentage increases, etc.) on listed companies only.

7. Other Rule 8(5) & Statutory Disclosures

The remaining checklist items cover conservation of energy/technology absorption/foreign exchange (Rule 8(3)), the web-link to the Annual Return in place of the erstwhile MGT-9 extract (Section 92(3) read with 134(3)(a), since the Companies (Amendment) Act, 2017), deposits accepted from the public (Section 73/76), significant or material orders by a Regulator/Court/Tribunal, the POSH Internal Committee disclosure (Rule 8(5)(x) of the Accounts Rules, read with the Sexual Harassment of Women at Workplace Act, 2013), and the AOC-1 statement for subsidiaries/associates/joint ventures (Section 129(3)).

8. Disclosures That Apply Only to Listed Companies

Corporate Governance Report, Management Discussion & Analysis, and the Business Responsibility and Sustainability Report (for the top 1,000 listed entities by market capitalisation) are SEBI (Listing Obligations and Disclosure Requirements) Regulations, 2015 requirements, not Companies Act requirements — but are conventionally bundled into a listed company's Annual Report alongside the Board's Report. Also listed-only are the Rule 5(1) ratio of remuneration to median employee remuneration, the statement on stock exchanges where the shares are listed together with high/low market price data, the Registrar & Share Transfer Agent's details, and the Prohibition of Insider Trading (PIT) Code compliance officer declaration under SEBI (PIT) Regulations, 2015. All are flagged separately in the checklist for that reason and are hidden automatically unless "Listed Company" is selected (or "Show all items" is switched on).

12. Form MGT-14 — Filing the Resolution That Approves This Report

The Board resolution by which the Directors approve the financial statements and the Board's Report itself is one of the thirteen matters listed in Section 179(3) of the Companies Act, 2013 — specifically clause (g), the power “to approve financial statement and the Board's report.” Section 117(1), read with Section 117(3)(g) and Rule 24 of the Companies (Management and Administration) Rules, 2014, requires that a certified true copy of this resolution be filed with the Registrar in e-Form MGT-14 within 30 days of the Board meeting at which it is passed. This obligation is separate from, and in addition to, filing the financial statements themselves in Form AOC-4 under Section 137, and the Annual Return in Form MGT-7/7A under Section 92 — all three are independent filings with independent deadlines and independent penalties.

Private company exemption: MCA Notification No. G.S.R. 464(E) dated 5 June 2015 exempts private companies (and, by extension, an OPC or a Section 8 company incorporated in the private form) from Section 117(3)(g). A private company's Board resolution approving the accounts and this report under Section 179(3) therefore does not need to be filed in MGT-14. The exemption is narrow — it covers only the Section 179(3) list (approving accounts/the report, borrowing, investing, granting loans/guarantees, diversifying the business, amalgamation/merger/reconstruction, taking over another company, political contributions, appointing/removing KMP, and the rest of that sub-section). It does not extend to special resolutions (Section 117(3)(a)), to the appointment or variation of terms of a managing director/whole-time director/manager (Section 117(3)(c)), or to any other agreement or resolution the Central Government separately prescribes under Section 117(3)(h) — a private company must still file MGT-14 for those, exactly like any other company.

The e-Form must be filed within 30 days of the resolution being passed; delay attracts an escalating additional fee under the Companies (Registration Offices and Fees) Rules, 2014, and once the delay exceeds 300 days from the date the resolution was passed, the form can no longer be filed on the ordinary portal at all — the company must first apply for condonation of delay to the Regional Director/NCLT (via Form CG-1) before MGT-14 can be accepted. Practically, this means a single missed MGT-14 for a Board's Report resolution can surface years later — commonly when a separate MCA filing (see the Nidhi-company examples in Section 13 below) is rejected for want of it — by which time the maximum penalty has typically accrued.

Penalty for default, under Section 117(2): the company is liable to a penalty of Rs. 1,00,000, plus a further Rs. 500 for every day the failure continues, subject to a maximum of Rs. 25,00,000; every officer in default (including a liquidator, if any) is liable to Rs. 50,000, plus Rs. 500/day continuing, subject to a maximum of Rs. 5,00,000 each. A Small Company, OPC, Start-up or Producer Company can have this penalty halved under Section 446B.

13. Punishment for Non-Compliance — Penalties & Illustrative Case Studies

The Companies (Amendment) Acts of 2019 and 2020 decriminalised most of the offences that attach to the Board's Report and its related filings, converting court-prosecuted fines into penalties that a Registrar of Companies, acting as Adjudicating Officer under Section 454 (read with the Companies (Adjudication of Penalties) Rules, 2014), can impose directly after a show-cause notice and hearing — without the company or its directors needing to be convicted in a criminal court. Lesser penalty — Section 446B: a Small Company, OPC, Start-up or Producer Company is liable to not more than one-half of the penalty specified for a default under Sections 92(5), 117(2), 137(3), and certain other sections. Section 134(8) is not itself listed in Section 446B, but several ROC adjudication orders (see below) have in practice extended this concession to Section 134(8) once the company is shown to meet the Small Company definition — always check the current text of Section 446B and the order being relied on before assuming this applies.

Illustrative Case Studies (Published ROC Adjudication Orders)

These are genuine orders passed by Registrars of Companies acting as Adjudicating Officers, summarised here for awareness only — each turns on its own facts and is available on the MCA adjudication-order archive.

  • Kejriwal Casting Ltd. — ROC West Bengal (2022): the company had not prepared any Board's Report at all for two financial years and filed a suo-motu application admitting the default. The flat Section 134(8) penalty was imposed for each year in default — Rs. 3,00,000 on the company and Rs. 50,000 on the Managing Director, for every year the report was missing.
  • ICL Mutual Benefits Corporation Ltd. — ROC Uttar Pradesh I (2026): the Directors' Report was never attached to the financial statements for FY 2017-18. After the company failed to respond to a show-cause notice, the Adjudicating Officer imposed Rs. 3,00,000 on the company and Rs. 50,000 each on four officers in default under Section 134(8).
  • Shreekrishna Impex Ventures Ltd. — ROC Gwalior (2026): the Board's Report omitted disclosure of director appointments/resignations, a related-party loan, and website-maintenance particulars. As the company met the Small Company definition, Section 446B relief was applied — Rs. 1,50,000 on the company and Rs. 25,000 on each officer, instead of the full Rs. 3,00,000/Rs. 50,000.
  • Mazhil Nidhi Ltd. (Section 134(1)): the financial statements and Board's Report attached to Form AOC-4 had not been signed by two directors as Section 134(1) requires. A total penalty of Rs. 4,50,000 was split between the company and its directors, since the company did not qualify for Section 446B relief.
  • MGT-14 non-filing surfacing via Form NDH-4 — a recurring Nidhi-company pattern (ROC Chennai, Ernakulam, Kanpur, 2025–26): several Nidhi companies discovered their MGT-14 default only when the Ministry rejected their Form NDH-4 (Nidhi-status declaration) for want of the MGT-14 that should have been filed against the Board resolution approving their accounts. Delays ranged from about 40 days to over 2,870 days after the resolution date, and penalties ranged upward to the statutory maximum — in one case Rs. 7,85,000 in aggregate on the company and its officers — illustrating how a single missed filing can resurface years later and, by then, attract close to the maximum penalty.
  • Innoctive Technologies Pvt. Ltd. — ROC Pune: a 40-day delay in filing MGT-14 for a special resolution was voluntarily disclosed by the company through a suo-motu application. Because the company qualified as both a Small Company and a recognised start-up, the Adjudicating Officer applied Section 446B and halved the Section 117(2) penalty that would otherwise have applied.
Practical takeaway: almost every order above began with an ordinary, easily-overlooked gap — a report not attached, a signature missing, a 30-day filing clock not diarised — rather than any deliberate wrongdoing, and several were only caught years later when an unrelated MCA filing was rejected. Diarise the MGT-14 and AOC-4/MGT-7 deadlines the moment this report is approved, and have the signed report, its annexures, and every filing checked by a practising Company Secretary before submission.
