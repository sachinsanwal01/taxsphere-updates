Minutes of Meeting — Compliance Guide

What Section 118 and the Secretarial Standards require, and what tends to go wrong — read this before entering any minutes drafted in the Generator tab into the Minutes Book.

Scope note: This guide summarises the standing law under Section 118 of the Companies Act, 2013, the Companies (Management and Administration) Rules, 2014, and Secretarial Standard‑1 (board/committee meetings) and Secretarial Standard‑2 (general meetings) issued by the ICSI. Provisions, formats and penalty amounts are amended from time to time — always verify the current position on mca.gov.in or with a practising Company Secretary before relying on any figure below.

1. What Minutes Are, and Why They Matter

Minutes are the official, written record of what a meeting of the Board (or of the members, in a general meeting) actually decided. They are distinct from a resolution: a resolution is the operative decision text, while minutes are the fuller record — who attended, what was tabled, what was discussed, and what was resolved, item by item, in the order the meeting took it up.

Under Section 118(6), minutes kept in accordance with the section are evidence of the proceedings recorded in them, and the meeting itself is presumed to have been duly called and held, and every resolution passed presumed validly passed, unless the contrary is proved. That evidentiary weight is exactly why the drafting, timing and signing of minutes is treated as a statutory obligation rather than a formality — a poorly kept Minutes Book can undermine the very decisions it is meant to record.

3. What the Minutes Must Contain

Section 118(10) requires every company to observe the applicable Secretarial Standards (SS‑1 for Board and committee meetings, SS‑2 for general meetings). Together with Section 118 itself, the minutes should be a fair and correct summary of the proceedings, and should ordinarily record:

  • The kind of meeting, the company's name, the day, date, time and venue.
  • The names of directors/members present (and, for a Board meeting, the mode of participation — physical or by video conferencing), and of those granted leave of absence.
  • The names of persons in attendance by invitation (auditors, professional advisors, the company secretary).
  • A record that the requisite quorum was present, and who chaired the meeting.
  • Confirmation of the minutes of the preceding meeting.
  • All appointments made at the meeting.
  • The exact text of every resolution passed, with the majority by which, or the manner in which, it was passed.
  • Where a director has recorded dissent or wishes a statement entered, that fact and, if requested, the text of the dissent.
  • The time at which the meeting commenced and concluded.

4. What Should Be Left Out

Section 118(5) gives the Chairman discretion, exercised reasonably, to exclude from the minutes any matter that in the Chairman's opinion is:

  • defamatory of any person; or
  • irrelevant or immaterial to the proceedings; or
  • detrimental to the interests of the company.

In practice, minutes record decisions, not a verbatim transcript of debate. SS‑1/SS‑2 both favour a concise summary of the discussion leading to each resolution over a blow-by-blow account of who said what — partly for exactly this reason, and partly because an overly detailed record can itself become a liability.

5. Minutes of Committee Meetings (Audit / NRC / SRC / CSR Committee)

Section 118(1) applies Section 118 to a meeting of any committee of the Board exactly as it applies to a Board meeting, and SS‑1 governs committee minutes on the same footing as Board minutes (separate Minutes Book, signature within 30 days, permanent preservation). The Act constitutes several committees by name — the Audit Committee and the Nomination & Remuneration Committee (Section 178), and the Corporate Social Responsibility Committee (Section 135) for companies that cross the applicable thresholds; the Board may also constitute a Stakeholders Relationship Committee where the company has more than 1,000 holders of shares, debentures or other securities (proviso to Section 178(5)).

A committee ordinarily recommends to the Board rather than finally deciding — e.g. the Audit Committee recommends financial statements and related-party transactions, the NRC recommends appointments and remuneration. Minute the recommendation and, separately, the Board's own resolution acting on it at its next meeting.

6. Class Meetings — Variation of Class Rights (Section 48)

Where a company has issued shares in different classes, the rights attached to any one class can only be varied with the consent of that class itself. Section 48(1) permits variation either by (a) obtaining the consent in writing of three-fourths of the issued shares of that class, or (b) a special resolution passed at a separate meeting of the holders of the issued shares of that class — the route this tool's "Class Meeting" tab drafts minutes for.

Section 48(2) gives shareholders holding not less than 10% of the issued shares of the class, who did not consent to or vote in favour of the variation, the right to apply to the National Company Law Tribunal to have the variation cancelled, within 21 days of the consent being given or the resolution being passed. The variation does not take effect until confirmed by the Tribunal if such an application is made. Section 48(5) applies the provisions of Sections 100 to 111 (notice, quorum, proxy, etc.) to a class meeting as they apply to a general meeting.

7. Postal Ballot (Section 110)

A postal ballot lets members vote on a resolution by post or electronically (remote e-voting), without a physical meeting being held. Section 110(1) empowers the Central Government to prescribe the class of companies that must transact specified business only by postal ballot; every other company may choose to transact any business by postal ballot except ordinary business and business where directors or auditors have a right to be heard. A postal ballot record has no quorum, chairman, or "present/attendance" concept — the minutes generated by this tool instead record the notice date, the last date for receipt of votes, the Scrutinizer appointed, and the result declared, together with the vote count for each resolution.

Under Section 110(1), a resolution passed by postal ballot is, for all purposes, treated as if it had been passed at a general meeting convened for that purpose — which is why it is still an Ordinary or Special Resolution depending on the majority obtained, and still needs Form MGT‑14 where a special resolution is involved.

8. Resolution by Circulation (Section 175)

Section 175(1) allows the Board (or a committee) to pass a resolution without a meeting: the draft, with the necessary papers, is circulated to all directors (or committee members) entitled to vote, at their registered addresses in India, and the resolution is passed if approved by a majority of those entitled to vote on it. The proviso to Section 175(1) is an important check: if not less than one-third of the total number of directors require that the resolution be decided only at a meeting, the Chairperson must put it to a meeting instead of circulation.

Section 175(2) requires that a resolution passed by circulation be noted at the Board's next meeting and recorded in the minutes of that meeting, together with the date of circulation, the date each director's response was received, and the date the resolution was deemed passed — the "Noted at the next Board meeting" field in this tool's Circulation tab captures that.

9. One Person Company (OPC) Resolutions (Section 122)

Section 122(1) exempts an OPC from Section 98 and Sections 100 to 111 of the Act — in other words, an OPC is not required to hold an Annual General Meeting, an Extraordinary General Meeting, or to comply with the usual notice/quorum/proxy machinery for general meetings at all.

In their place, Section 122(3) provides that where the Act requires a resolution to be passed by the members of a company in general meeting, it is sufficient for the resolution to be communicated by the sole member to the company and entered in the minutes book required under Section 118, signed and dated by the member — such a resolution is deemed to have been passed at a general meeting. Section 122(4) applies the same mechanism where the company has only one director: a resolution required to be passed by the Board is sufficient if communicated by that sole director to the company and entered in the minutes book, signed and dated, and it is deemed to have been passed at a Board meeting duly convened and held.

Both routes are still subject to the general Section 118 timeline — entry in the minutes book within 30 days — which is why this tool's OPC tab asks for both the date the resolution was passed/communicated and the date it was entered in the minutes book.

11. Penalty for Default

Under Section 118(11), if a company fails to comply with the requirements of Section 118 regarding the keeping and signing of minutes, the company is liable to a penalty of ₹25,000, and every officer of the company who is in default is liable to a penalty of ₹5,000. These are civil penalties (as recast after decriminalisation of several Companies Act provisions), and are separate from the criminal offence discussed below. This applies equally to Board, committee, general, and class meeting minutes, and to the minutes book maintained for postal ballot, circular and OPC resolutions.

Tampering with minutes — a distinct, more serious offence

Section 118(12) treats any person found guilty of tampering with the minutes of proceedings much more seriously — punishable with imprisonment for a term which may extend to two years, and with a fine of not less than ₹25,000, extending up to ₹1,00,000. This underscores why minutes, once entered and signed, are not to be altered.
