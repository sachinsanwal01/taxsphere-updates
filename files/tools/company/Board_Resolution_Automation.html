Board Resolution — Compliance Guide

Overview, timelines and filing obligations under the Companies Act, 2013 — read this before relying on any resolution drafted in the Generator tab.

Scope note: This guide summarises the standing law under the Companies Act, 2013, the Companies (Meetings of Board and its Powers) Rules, 2014, and Secretarial Standard‑1 (SS‑1) issued by the ICSI. Provisions, thresholds and penalty amounts are amended from time to time — always verify the current position on mca.gov.in or with a practising Company Secretary before relying on any figure below for an actual filing.

1. Overview & Concept

A board resolution is the formal record of a decision taken by a company's Board of Directors, acting collectively as the company's management under Section 179 of the Companies Act, 2013. The Board manages the company on behalf of its shareholders, and every significant act — appointing a director, opening a bank account, borrowing money, allotting shares — must be traceable to a specific decision the Board actually took, recorded in a specific form, at a specific time. A resolution is that record.

Resolutions are not all the same weight. A distinction runs through company law between matters the Board can decide on its own (a board resolution) and matters that additionally need the shareholders' approval (an ordinary or special resolution passed at a general meeting). This guide focuses on board-level resolutions — when they're required, how they must be passed, and what has to be filed afterwards.

A board resolution can be passed in one of two ways: at a duly convened meeting of the Board (physical or via video conferencing) under Section 173, or by circulation under Section 175, where a draft is circulated to every director instead of holding a meeting. Not every matter is eligible for the circulation route — see Section 5 below.

2. Types of Resolutions Under the Companies Act, 2013

"Resolution" is a broader term than "board resolution." The Act recognises several kinds, distinguished by who passes them and what majority is needed. Getting the type wrong — passing something as a board resolution when the Act requires shareholder approval, or as an ordinary resolution when a special resolution is required — makes the underlying action legally defective, however well the meeting itself was conducted.

Special Resolution — the procedural requirements

  • The intention to move it as a special resolution must be stated in the notice convening the general meeting.
  • The general meeting itself needs 21 clear days' notice (shorter notice is possible only with the consent of members holding the requisite majority in number and voting power).
  • An explanatory statement under Section 102 must accompany the notice, setting out the material facts and the nature of any director/manager's concern or interest.
  • Voting must show at least 3 times as many votes in favour as against, among members present (in person, by proxy, or by postal ballot) and voting.
  • Unlike many board resolutions, a special resolution must always be filed in Form MGT-14 within 30 days of being passed — there is no private-company exemption for special resolutions.

3. When a Board Resolution Has to Be Passed

As a working rule, a board resolution is required whenever the Board exercises one of its statutory powers, or whenever the Articles of Association or a specific section of the Act calls for "the approval of the Board." 5. Resolution by Circulation (Section 175) — and What Can't Be Circulated

Where a decision is urgent and can't wait for the next scheduled meeting, the Board may pass it by circulation instead: the draft resolution and supporting papers are sent to every director at their registered address, and it's treated as passed once approved by a majority of the directors entitled to vote on it. If at least one-third of the directors ask for it to be decided at a meeting instead, it must go to a meeting. A circular resolution must be noted at the Board's next meeting and entered into the minutes of that meeting.

Not everything qualifies. Under Section 179(3) read with Rule 8 of the Companies (Meetings of Board and its Powers) Rules, 2014, the following matters can only be decided at a meeting of the Board and can never be passed by circulation:

  • Making calls on shareholders for unpaid share money
  • Authorising buy-back of securities under Section 68
  • Issuing securities, including debentures, whether in or outside India
  • Borrowing money (otherwise than on debentures)
  • Investing the funds of the company
  • Granting loans, or giving a guarantee or providing security, in respect of loans
  • Approving the financial statements and the Board's Report
  • Diversifying the business of the company
  • Approving an amalgamation, merger or reconstruction
  • Taking over, or acquiring a controlling or substantial stake in, another company
  • Any other matter prescribed under Rule 8, including takeover of a listed/unlisted company and related-party contracts above the prescribed threshold

In practice: allotment of shares, availing a loan, and creation of a charge — three of the resolution types in this Generator — fall in this restricted list and must therefore be passed at an actual Board meeting, not by circulation, even in an urgent situation.

6. Minutes (Section 118)

  • Every resolution passed — at a meeting or by circulation — must be recorded in the Minutes Book within 30 days of the meeting (or of the resolution being noted, for a circular resolution).
  • Minutes must be signed by the Chairperson of that meeting, or by the Chairperson of the next meeting.
  • Minutes are treated as conclusive evidence of the proceedings, unless successfully challenged; they should record the names of directors present, the decisions taken, and the exact wording of each resolution passed.
  • Minute books must be preserved permanently and kept at the registered office (or another approved place), open to inspection by directors.

7. Filing with the Registrar — Form MGT-14 (Section 117)

Certain board resolutions don't just need to be minuted — they need to be filed with the Registrar of Companies in Form MGT-14, within 30 days of the resolution being passed (counted from the date of the meeting, not the date the minutes are signed).

The private-company exemption is narrow and conditional (broadly: the company must not be in default of its own AOC-4/MGT-7 filings). Where a company's compliance history is uncertain, it's safer to have a company secretary confirm the exemption applies rather than assume it.
