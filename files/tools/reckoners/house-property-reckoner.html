RECKONER

Income from House Property

Annual value, deductions, and computation for self-occupied, let-out and deemed let-out property
Sections 22–27
Scope note: covers computation of income from house property under both tax regimes, and how far a resulting loss can be set off or carried forward under each — see the Loss Set-Off tab for the old-vs-new regime split.

What counts, and who it's taxed on

House property income is charged not on rent alone, but on the property's annual value — a notional figure the law constructs even when a property earns no rent at all.

Three things every computation needs

  1. Establish ownershipThe legal or deemed owner is taxed — not the occupant, and not whoever collects the rent unless they are also the owner.
  2. Classify the propertySelf-occupied (up to two), let-out, or deemed let-out (third property onward) — each follows a different annual value rule.
  3. Apply the deductionsA flat 30% standard deduction, and interest on borrowed capital — capped at ₹2,00,000 only where the property is self-occupied.

Scope — who's taxed, and what "rent" actually covers

Only the owner (or deemed owner) is taxed under this head, and only for a building or land appurtenant to it — vacant land on its own falls outside this head entirely. Rent collected by anyone else on the property never falls under "Income from house property" — most commonly, a tenant's sub-letting income is taxed as Other Sources or business income instead, never under this head, since the sub-lessor isn't the owner.

Composite rent — where the owner charges one combined amount for the building plus other assets (furniture) or services (lift, security, water) — has to be split. If the building and the other component are let inseparably (an equipped theatre, a serviced office), the entire receipt falls outside this head altogether — it's Business Income or Other Sources, not House Property. If they're separable (a bungalow let along with a refrigerator), the rent is apportioned: the building's share stays under this head, the rest goes to Business Income/Other Sources — even if the owner actually billed it as one figure.

A short list of income that's exempt from this head altogether

Beyond the computation rules, a handful of specific categories are carved out entirely — house property income that never enters the computation at all:

  • Farm buildings on or near agricultural land Sec 2(5) (old Sec 2(1A)) — treated as agricultural income and exempt.
  • Trust property Sec 332–350 & Sch XVI (old Sec 11) — income applied for charitable or religious purposes; the new Act gives charitable/religious trust taxation a dedicated block of sections plus a schedule, rather than the single old Sec 11.
  • One palace of a former ruler Sch III (39) (old Sec 10(19A)), and property income of local authorities Sch III (22) (old Sec 10(20)).
  • Qualifying educational or medical institutions Sch VII (old Sec 10(23C)), and a registered trade union's property income Sch III (31) (old Sec 10(24)).

These sit outside the main computation chain covered in the rest of this tool and mostly apply to specific institutional assessees rather than an individual taxpayer's own residence or investment property.

Interest relief — the self-occupied cap, and why it depends on regime

Interest on a housing loan is deductible in full for let-out property in both regimes. For a self-occupied property, the treatment is regime-dependent, not just capped:

Old Regime Capped at ₹2,00,000 under Sec 22(1)(b) (old Sec 24(b)) — any interest above that gives no tax benefit in the year it is paid.

New Regime Interest deduction on a self-occupied property is not available at all — not ₹2,00,000, not any amount. A self-occupied property always returns Nil income in the new regime; it can never generate a loss.

Old Regime vs New Regime — the three things that change

The new tax regime (Sec 202, default from Tax Year 2026-27) is the default unless you actively opt for the old regime. It changes house property computation and loss usage in three specific ways.

Practical effect: a leveraged self-occupied property gives zero tax relief under the new regime, and any loss a let-out property does generate is usable only within the same tax year against other house property income — it cannot shelter salary or business income, and nothing survives into next year.

Pre-construction interest — deducted later, in 5 equal instalments

Interest that accrues before a property is completed or acquired isn't lost — it's banked and released later, but only for loans taken to purchase or construct a property. It does not apply to a loan for repairs, renewal, or reconstruction.

  1. Pre-construction periodRuns from the date the loan is borrowed to the end of the financial year immediately before the year construction is completed (or the property is acquired) — or the date the loan is repaid, if that comes first.
  2. No deduction during this periodInterest accruing in these years cannot be claimed as it arises; it is simply totalled up and held.
  3. Released in 5 equal annual instalmentsStarting from the year construction is completed / possession is taken, one-fifth of the accumulated pre-construction interest is added to that year's regular interest deduction — every year for 5 years.

This add-on still has to fit inside whatever cap otherwise applies to the property — see below for self-occupied, and it's uncapped for let-out/deemed let-out in both regimes.

Interest is deductible on accrual basis — payable, not necessarily paid

Sec 22(1)(b)/24(b) uses the word "payable," and that's deliberate: the deduction is available for interest that has accrued for the year, whether or not it's actually been remitted to the lender — a certificate from the lender showing interest payable is enough. This isn't a special concession for any one category of borrower; it applies across self-occupied, let-out, and deemed let-out property alike.

Two things it does not stretch to cover: interest on a fresh loan taken purely to repay/refinance the original housing loan is still deductible, but ancillary charges — processing fees, brokerage, commission, and any penal interest on a missed instalment — are not treated as "interest" for this deduction at all.

The self-occupied cap isn't always ₹2,00,000 — sometimes it's ₹30,000

Under the old regime, the ₹2,00,000 ceiling on self-occupied interest under Sec 22(1)(b) (old Sec 24(b)) — current year's interest plus any pre-construction instalment together — only applies if both conditions below are met. Otherwise the cap drops to ₹30,000:

₹2,00,000 cap applies

Loan taken for purchase or construction, AND the purchase/construction is completed within 5 years from the end of the financial year in which the loan was taken.

₹30,000 cap applies instead

Loan taken for repair, renewal, or reconstruction — always ₹30,000, regardless of timeline. Or a purchase/construction loan where completion takes longer than 5 years.

New Regime None of this matters under the new regime — self-occupied interest is Nil either way, whatever the loan purpose or completion timeline. The ₹2,00,000 / ₹30,000 distinction is an old-regime concept only.

For let-out and deemed let-out property, there is no ₹30,000/₹2,00,000 distinction at all — interest for any of these purposes (purchase, construction, repair, renewal, reconstruction), including the pre-construction instalment, is fully deductible with no cap, in both regimes.

Unrealised rent — two different rules, don't confuse them

At GAV stage If the tenant simply hasn't paid the rent for the current year, that unrealised amount is deducted from actual rent before comparing it with Expected Rent to arrive at GAV — but only if all four conditions hold: the tenancy is bona fide, the defaulting tenant has vacated (or steps have been taken to make him), he isn't occupying any other property of the owner's, and the owner has taken all steps to recover the amount, including legal proceedings, or can satisfy the AO that proceedings would be futile.

Sec 23 (old Sec 25A) — the arrears/unrealised-rent provision; not to be confused with old Sec 23 itself (the general annual-value provision, which maps to new Sec 21). If that same unrealised rent — or arrears of rent from an earlier year — is later recovered, it's taxed in the year of receipt, after a flat 30% deduction, whether or not the assessee still owns the property that year. This is a separate, later-year event from the GAV-stage adjustment above; the two should never be netted against each other.

Deemed owner — the fuller list

Beyond the spouse/minor-child transfer and co-op society allottee already covered, two more routes create deemed ownership — full detail in the Sections of Law tab:

Impartible estate holder

Deemed owner of the property comprised in the estate — an indivisible holding such as terrace rights is the classic example.

Possession under TPA Sec 53A

A written agreement, consideration paid or willing to be paid, and possession taken — deemed owner even without a registered sale deed.

And the spouse/minor-child transfer carries two carve-outs worth remembering: it does not apply to a transfer to a spouse under an agreement to live apart, or to a married daughter — either exception takes the transferor out of Sec 25(a)/27(i) entirely.

Stock-in-trade property — Nil value while unsold, but only for 2 years

A property (or part) that a builder/developer holds as stock-in-trade and hasn't let out gets a Nil annual value — but this concession runs out 2 years from the end of the financial year in which the completion certificate was obtained. Once that window closes, an unsold, unlet unit reverts to the ordinary rules — typically taxed as deemed let-out like any other unrented property the builder owns.

Split scenarios — part-year letting, and split-unit properties

Let out for part of the year, self-occupied for the rest: the property is still treated as let-out for the whole year when computing GAV — Expected Rent is taken for all 12 months — but actual rent is counted only for the months it was actually let. This does not automatically bring in vacancy relief: GAV for that year is still the higher of prorated Expected Rent and actual rent received, the same comparison as any let-out property. Vacancy relief (GAV = actual rent, even if lower than Expected Rent) is a distinct, narrower rule that applies only where the property was held out to let throughout but a genuine vacancy — not finding a tenant — pulled actual rent below Expected Rent for that reason specifically.

One property, two units, different uses: where a house is physically split — one unit self-occupied, the other let out or used for another purpose — the two units are treated as two independent properties, each computed under its own category rather than as a single blended figure.

Sec 20 through 25 (2025 Act) — with the old-Act numbers you'll still use for filings up to Tax Year 2025-26

Each section governs one link in the computation chain — from establishing the charge to defining who is treated as an owner even without a registered title. Headings below lead with the 2025 Act number since that's what applies from Tax Year 2026-27 onward; the old Sec 22–27 numbering in brackets is what you'll still see on any return for FY 2025-26 (AY 2026-27) or earlier.

Sec 20 (old Sec 22) Basis of Charge

Charges the annual value of any building or land appurtenant to it, owned by the assessee — unless it is used for the assessee's own business or profession, in which case it falls outside this head entirely.

Sec 21 (old Sec 23) Annual Value

Defines Gross Annual Value as the higher of expected rent (municipal value or fair rent, capped by standard rent) and actual rent received, with relief for genuine vacancy. Prescribes Nil value for up to two self-occupied properties.

Sec 22 (old Sec 24) Deductions

Allows two deductions from Net Annual Value: a flat 30% standard deduction under Sec 22(1)(a) (old Sec 24(a)), and interest on borrowed capital under Sec 22(1)(b) (old Sec 24(b)) — capped at ₹2,00,000 for self-occupied property under the old regime. Sec 202 (old Sec 115BAC) withdraws the self-occupied interest deduction entirely under the new regime, and separately blocks inter-head set-off and carry-forward of any house property loss — see the Loss Set-Off tab.

Old Sec 25 — Amounts Not Deductible (absorbed into new Sec 22, not a standalone new section)

Disallows interest payable outside India unless tax has been deducted at source on it, or a person in India has been appointed to account for the tax.

Sec 23 (old Sec 25A) Arrears of Rent & Unrealised Rent Received Subsequently

Where arrears of rent, or previously-unrealised rent, are received in a later tax year, the amount is charged to tax in the year of receipt — after a flat 30% deduction, and regardless of whether the assessee still owns the property in that year. This is a distinct mechanism from the vacancy/unrealised-rent adjustment made while computing GAV in the year the rent falls due — see the Concepts tab for how the two differ.

Sec 24 (old Sec 26) Co-owned Property

Where a property has definite, ascertainable co-owner shares, each co-owner's share of income is computed separately rather than taxing the property as one unit.

Sec 25 (old Sec 27) Deemed Owner

Extends "owner" beyond the registered title-holder to cover:

  • A transferor who transfers a property to a spouse (other than under an agreement to live apart) or to a minor child (other than a married daughter) without adequate consideration.
  • The holder of an impartible estate, for the property comprised in it — an indivisible holding such as terrace rights is the classic example.
  • A member of a co-operative society, company, or other AOP to whom a building (or part of one) is allotted or leased under a house-building scheme.
  • A person in possession of a property under Sec 53A of the Transfer of Property Act, 1882 — a written agreement, consideration paid or the purchaser willing to pay it, and possession taken in pursuance of the agreement — even where no registered conveyance exists.
  • A lessee under a lease of not less than 12 years (whether originally fixed or extendable to that length). A month-to-month tenancy, or any lease of 1 year or less, does not attract this provision.

How much house property loss can you set off in your ITR?

A house property loss moves through up to three stages — intra-head, inter-head, then carry forward. Under the old regime all three are open, subject to the ₹2,00,000 inter-head cap. Under the new regime (the default), only the first stage is open — the loss cannot cross into other heads, and nothing survives to next year.

The three stages, and which regime keeps each one open

  1. Intra-head set-off Sec 108 (old Sec 70) — loss from one property is first set off against income from another house property you own, in the same year — no cap, open under both regimes since this is basic computation of the head figure, not a set-off Sec 115BAC/Sec 202 touches.
  2. Inter-head set-off Sec 109 (old Sec 71(3A)) Old only — if the head still shows a net loss, it can be set off against salary, business, or other-source income — capped at ₹2,00,000 per year, and available only under the old regime. Not available under the new regime — the loss cannot touch any other head, not even up to ₹2,00,000.
  3. Carry forward Sec 110 (old Sec 71B) Old only — whatever remains unabsorbed after stages 1–2 carries forward for up to 8 tax years, settable only against future house property income — but this route too is old-regime only. Under the new regime, any house property loss left after intra-head adjustment simply lapses at year end — it cannot be carried forward at all. This applies equally to a loss arising in the current year and to a loss already brought forward from an earlier year: the restriction is keyed to the regime chosen for the year the set-off is attempted, not to when the loss originally arose. A pre-1 April 2026 loss still carried forward under the Sec 536(2)(m)/(n) transitional rule can likewise only be set off in a tax year where the old regime is elected — see the Old → New Sections tab.

Old Regime vs New Regime — loss set-off at a glance

New tax regime: only the intra-head route survives

Under the new tax regime (default from Tax Year 2026-27), house property loss cannot be set off against any other head of income — not even up to ₹2,00,000 — and cannot be carried forward to a future tax year. The only relief available in the year the loss arises is netting it against income from another house property. Any balance beyond that gives no further tax benefit and simply disappears at year end.

Eleven planning propositions worth knowing

These are established interpretive positions on the house property head — some settled by the courts, some simply a matter of reading the deeming, clubbing and TDS provisions closely. None of them override the computation rules in the earlier tabs; they're about which facts to arrange, which elections to make, and which compliance step not to skip.

Elections that change the numbers

"I live there" doesn't automatically make it self-occupied

It's a common assumption that occupation alone decides the self-occupied label. Not quite — occupation is necessary, but the Nil-value exemption itself is capped at two houses of the owner's own choosing, however many he actually lives in over the year. A third or fourth residence he genuinely occupies still falls into deemed let-out and gets taxed on notional rent, occupation notwithstanding.

Practical takeaway: work out the notional Expected Rent for each residence before nominating the two self-occupied ones, and treat "highest notional value" as a starting heuristic, not a guaranteed answer — a careless nomination (say, simply the first two purchased) can cost real tax for no legal reason, but the actual best choice depends on municipal taxes, interest, and loss-set-off effects too, so run the full computation property-by-property before finalizing.

Co-ownership doesn't just split the EMI — it multiplies the deductions

Where a property is co-owned with definite, ascertainable shares, income is computed separately for each co-owner under Sec 24 (old Sec 26), not once for the property as a whole. If the co-owners are also co-borrowers — legally liable on the loan, not merely named on the property deed — each independently repaying their own share, each one separately claims their own interest deduction — up to ₹2,00,000 apiece for a self-occupied property under the old regime — and their own 30% standard deduction on their share of Net Annual Value.

Practical takeaway: two co-owners genuinely servicing the loan together can shelter up to ₹4,00,000 of interest between them rather than ₹2,00,000 for a single owner. This depends on ownership share, the borrowing arrangement, legal liability for the loan, and documented evidence of payment lining up — a co-owner who isn't actually paying their share of the EMI can't claim a deduction for interest they didn't pay, however the ownership deed reads.

An unused third property is taxed whether or not you ever rent it out

Beyond the first two self-occupied houses, every additional residence is deemed let-out — taxed on notional Expected Rent — regardless of whether it's actually rented. "I'm not renting it out, so there's no income" isn't how the computation works; the law charges the notional figure either way.

Practical takeaway: before deciding to hold a third house vacant — for a child's future use, sentimental value, or any other reason — price in this notional tax cost as a genuine carrying expense. Actually letting it out at least brings in rent to offset the same tax bill; leaving it empty pays the tax with nothing to show for it.

Deemed ownership & family transfers

Co-op society allottees don't need a sale deed at all

Ownership under this head doesn't wait for a registered conveyance. A member of a co-operative society, company, or AOP allotted a building (or part of one) under a house-building scheme may be treated as deemed owner from the date of that allotment — well before any sale deed exists — and the interest they pay on outstanding instalments of the building's cost deductible under Sec 22(1)(b) (old Sec 24(b)) from that same date, same as any other owner. This turns on the allotment being definite rather than provisional: the commencement date for ownership and the interest deduction should be checked against the actual allotment terms, possession status, and payment obligations, not assumed from the scheme type alone.

Practical takeaway: a member's claim is easy to miss entirely on the mistaken assumption that no sale deed means no ownership yet. But don't over-correct either — verify the allotment is definite (not a mere proposal or waitlisted application) and check the supporting documents before relying on the allotment date. Separately, remember that interest under Sec 22(1)(b)/24(b) is on an accrual basis for every owner generally, not something specific to co-op allottees — see the Concepts tab.

Cash gift to a spouse: one trap avoided, one left standing

Gift cash to a wife and let her buy a house with it, and the husband may avoid deemed ownership of that property under Sec 25(a) (old Sec 27(i)) on those specific facts. That's often taken to mean "the property is now hers, nothing more to think about" — it isn't that simple. The clubbing provision is separate and still bites: her income from that property (or Nil, if she lives in it under Sec 21(6)/old Sec 23(2)) is added straight back into his total income under Sec 99(1)(a)(ii) (old Sec 64(1)(iv)) — and questions of source-tracing and genuineness of the gift remain relevant on top of that.

Practical takeaway: the cash-gift route only ever deals with deemed ownership, never with clubbing. To actually keep the income out of his hands, the asset transferred needs to be something other than house property altogether — and even then, the facts and documentation of the transfer matter.

The same escape doesn't repeat itself for a son's wife — but the outcome can still work in your favour

Transfer a house to a son's wife without consideration, and the transferor may likewise avoid deemed ownership under Sec 25(a) on those facts. But the rental income she earns from it is clubbed straight back into the transferor's hands under Sec 99(1)(b) (old Sec 64(1)(vi)) — the transferee is treated as owner for computing the figure, which is then added to the transferor's total income.

Practical takeaway: the outcome hinges entirely on what she does with it afterward. If she occupies it herself, annual value is Nil and there's nothing left to club on those facts — the transfer can work well in that specific pattern. Let her rent it out instead, and the clubbed income defeats the whole purpose. Either way, this is a fact-sensitive position, not a guaranteed result.

Payment timing & TDS compliance

Accrual doesn't count for municipal tax — only payment does

A municipal tax bill that's due but sitting unpaid at year-end earns nothing. The deduction only opens once the tax is actually paid, and only in the tax year it's paid in — due-basis or accrual-basis accounting has no bearing here. This deduction is taken from GAV to arrive at NAV, and only where the tax is genuinely borne by the owner, not the tenant.

Practical takeaway: a bill falling due in late March can be settled before 31 March rather than in April to pull the deduction a full year forward — the expense is identical either way, so this is purely a timing choice, worth weighing against the owner's own cash flow rather than treating as automatically worthwhile.

Interest paid abroad — TDS is a condition, not a formality

Interest payable outside India can be disallowed in full — not reduced, not prorated — where tax hasn't been deducted at source on it and there's no one in India who can be treated as an agent for the lender under Sec 306 (old Sec 163). Partial compliance buys no partial relief here. The applicable withholding provision and rate depend on the nature of the payment, the lender's residential status, and any treaty relief — provisions outside this chapter, so treat this as a flag to check rather than a self-contained TDS computation.

Practical takeaway: before claiming interest paid to a foreign lender or NRI under Sec 22(1)(b), confirm the TDS was actually deducted on the remittance — this is a condition the claim has to satisfy, not paperwork to tidy up later.

TDS on rent — the ₹50,000 threshold vanishes for an NRI landlord

The familiar rule — no TDS below ₹50,000 rent a month, under Sec 194-IB, for a resident landlord — simply doesn't carry over when the landlord is a non-resident. Rent paid to an NRI landlord instead falls under Sec 195, which has no monetary threshold at all — TDS applies from the first rupee of rent. The rate itself isn't a fixed number: it depends on the nature of the payment, surcharge, health and education cess, any applicable tax treaty, and whether the landlord holds a lower or nil deduction certificate — so it has to be worked out on the facts each time, not assumed.

Practical takeaway: confirm a landlord's residential status before assuming the ₹50,000 threshold protects a tenant from any TDS obligation — that threshold simply doesn't apply once the landlord is an NRI. Getting this wrong exposes the tenant to being treated as an assessee-in-default for the shortfall, with interest and a possible penalty on top; check the current Sec 195 rate, treaty position, and certificate status for the specific case rather than relying on a rule of thumb.

Interactions with other provisions

HRA and home loan interest are not an either/or choice

A surprisingly common belief is that claiming HRA rules out a home loan interest deduction in the same year, or vice versa — as if the two compete for the same rupee. They don't. HRA exemption (Sch III Sl. 11, old Sec 10(13A)) relates to rent actually paid for the accommodation lived in; interest deduction under Sec 22(1)(b)/24(b) relates to a property owned. Nothing in the Act ties one to the other.

Practical takeaway: this comes up most often where someone owns a home in one city (financed by a loan) but rents elsewhere for work, or owns a home in the same city that genuinely isn't suitable to occupy. Both benefits can be claimed together, old regime only, provided each is independently substantiated — rent receipts and a credible reason the owned house isn't occupied, on top of the usual loan interest certificate.

Legacy top-up: check for an old Sec 80EE / 80EEA claim before writing it off

Two now-closed schemes give a home-loan borrower an interest deduction over and above the Sec 24(b)/22(1)(b) figure, old regime only: old Sec 80EE — up to ₹50,000, for loans sanctioned 1-4-2016 to 31-3-2017 (loan ≤ ₹35 lakh, property value ≤ ₹50 lakh, no other residential property owned on the sanction date); and old Sec 80EEA — up to ₹1,50,000, for loans sanctioned 1-4-2019 to 31-3-2022 (stamp duty value ≤ ₹45 lakh, same no-other-property condition, and not eligible under 80EE). Both sanction windows are shut for new loans, but a borrower who took a qualifying loan back then and is still repaying it can keep claiming the top-up every year until the loan closes.

Practical takeaway: for any client still servicing a home loan sanctioned in either window, check whether this top-up is being claimed year after year — it's easy to miss precisely because the scheme itself is no longer open to new borrowers. These two sections haven't been separately cross-mapped to 2025 Act numbering in this tool yet; verify the new citation before quoting it in an opinion.

These are illustrative planning propositions, not guaranteed outcomes — they turn on the specific facts of each case.

Old Act (1961) → New Act (2025)

The Income-tax Act, 2025 replaced the Income-tax Act, 1961 from 1 April 2026. Section numbers changed across the board, but the house property rules themselves are carried over largely unchanged.

Which one applies to you: your ITR for FY 2025-26 (AY 2026-27), filed in July 2026, still uses the old section numbers 22–27. The new numbers below apply from Tax Year 2026-27 onward — the return you'll file in July 2027.

What actually changed

  • Numbering only, as between the two Acts — the 30% standard deduction, the GAV mechanism, and the ₹2,00,000 interest cap for self-occupied property (under the old regime) are unchanged in substance.
  • "Previous Year" and "Assessment Year" are replaced by a single concept: Tax Year.
  • The real substantive change sits outside the 1961→2025 renumbering — it comes from Sec 202 (old Sec 115BAC). Under the new tax regime (default from Tax Year 2026-27), self-occupied interest relief is not available at all, and house property loss can no longer be set off against other heads or carried forward — see the Loss Set-Off tab.
