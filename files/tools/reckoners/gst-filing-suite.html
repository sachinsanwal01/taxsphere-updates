Late fee is charged per day under both CGST and SGST, capped by turnover slab: ₹500 for nil returns, ₹2,000 up to ₹1.5cr turnover, ₹5,000 for ₹1.5cr–₹5cr, and ₹10,000 above ₹5cr (aggregate of both Acts). Interest runs from the day after the due date until payment, and is not capped. GSTR-3B cannot be filed at all beyond 3 years from its original due date.

What GST is

A destination-based, multi-stage, value-added consumption tax

The Goods and Services Tax replaced a fragmented indirect-tax system — excise, VAT, service tax, entry tax and more — with a single tax levied at every stage of the supply chain, on the value added at that stage, with credit for tax already paid on inputs. It is a destination-based tax: revenue accrues to the state where goods or services are ultimately consumed, not where they are produced.

The dual structure

CGSTCentral GST

Levied by the Centre on intra-state supplies, under the CGST Act, 2017.

SGST/UTGSTState / UT GST

Levied by the State or UT on the same intra-state supply, under the respective SGST/UTGST Act.

IGSTIntegrated GST

Levied by the Centre on inter-state supplies and imports, under the IGST Act, 2017 — later apportioned between Centre and destination State.

CessCompensation Cess

Levied on select goods to fund state revenue-loss compensation; scrapped for most items from 22 September 2025, retained only on a few tobacco-related products until legacy loan obligations are cleared.

Input Tax Credit — the mechanism that avoids cascading

A registered person can set off the GST paid on purchases ("input tax") against the GST payable on sales ("output tax"), so tax is effectively borne only on the value added at each stage. Credit utilisation follows a prescribed order — IGST first, then CGST, then SGST/UTGST — and is subject to conditions under Section 16 and restrictions under Section 17(5) (see the Input Tax Credit tab).

GST 2.0 — the September 2025 rate reform

The most significant rate overhaul since GST's 2017 launch, effective 22 September 2025

The 56th GST Council meeting (3 September 2025) collapsed the earlier five-tier structure (0%, 5%, 12%, 18%, 28%) into a simplified structure, abolishing the 12% and 28% slabs and introducing a new 40% de-merit rate.

Nil-rated
0%

Essential foods, most drugs and medicines on the exempt list, individual health & life insurance, educational materials.

Merit rate
5%

Daily essentials, agricultural goods, healthcare equipment — almost everything that used to sit in the old 12% slab moved here.

Standard rate
18%

The default rate for most goods and services — small cars, electronics, appliances, and around 90% of what used to be taxed at 28%.

De-merit / luxury
40%

Pan masala, aerated and caffeinated beverages, luxury vehicles and other sin/luxury goods — replacing the earlier 28% + cess combination.

Tobacco, pan masala and related products remain on the earlier 28% + compensation cess structure until the Centre's compensation-cess loan obligations to states are fully discharged, expected to complete by around December 2025 — after which they too move to the 40% slab.

ITC reversal is required only where a supply became genuinely exempt from 22 September 2025 — a plain rate reduction (e.g. 12% to 5%) does not by itself trigger reversal.

QRMP — Quarterly Return, Monthly Payment

Available to taxpayers with turnover up to ₹5 crore in the preceding financial year

QRMP lets small and mid-sized taxpayers file GSTR-1 and GSTR-3B once a quarter instead of monthly, while still paying tax every month so government cash flow isn't disrupted.

How the monthly payment works

Fixed Sum Method
  • Pay 35% of the previous quarter's cash-ledger tax liability for each of the first two months.
  • No interest is charged for those two months even if actual liability is higher — provided the 35% is deposited by the 25th.
Self-Assessment Method
  • Compute actual tax liability for the month based on real sales and available ITC.
  • Deposit that amount via Form PMT-06 by the 25th of the following month.

Invoice Furnishing Facility (IFF)

QRMP taxpayers can optionally upload B2B invoices for the first two months of the quarter through the IFF, so their buyers can claim ITC without waiting for the quarterly GSTR-1. This is optional — GSTR-1 for the quarter still covers all invoices for the full three months.

Opting in and out

  • Opt-in is done on the GST portal, typically in the first month of a quarter, and remains in effect until changed.
  • A taxpayer can switch between monthly and QRMP filing at the start of any quarter, subject to the turnover eligibility test at that time.
  • GSTR-1 and GSTR-3B for the quarter are still due by the 13th and 22nd/24th respectively, per the calendar in the previous tab.

    • Late fees, interest & other consequences

    Section 47 (late fee) and Section 50 (interest), CGST Act, 2017

    Late fee structure

    CategoryPer-day feeCap
    Nil return₹20/day (₹10 CGST + ₹10 SGST)₹500
    Turnover up to ₹1.5 crore₹50/day (₹25 + ₹25)₹2,000
    Turnover ₹1.5 crore – ₹5 crore₹50/day (₹25 + ₹25)₹5,000
    Turnover above ₹5 crore₹50/day (₹25 + ₹25)₹10,000

    Late fee applies to GSTR-1, GSTR-3B, GSTR-4, GSTR-5, GSTR-6 and GSTR-9, among others, and must be paid in cash — it cannot be settled using ITC. The caps above reflect the standard structure currently in force but have been revised by notification before, so verify against the current one for the specific return and period.

    Interest

  • 18% per annum on tax paid late — computed on the net cash tax liability, from the day after the due date until payment.
  • 24% per annum where excess Input Tax Credit was claimed or output tax liability was under-reported and later corrected.

    • Other consequences of non-filing

  • GSTR-1 filing is blocked if GSTR-3B for the preceding period is pending.
  • E-way bill generation can be blocked for consistent non-filers.
  • GSTR-3B cannot be filed at all once 3 years have passed from its original due date — the tax period is permanently locked.
  • Registration is liable to cancellation for continuous default in filing returns.

    • Input Tax Credit essentials

    Section 16 (eligibility) and Section 17(5) (blocked credits)

    Conditions to claim ITC — Section 16

  • Possession of a valid tax invoice or debit note.
  • The goods or services have actually been received.
  • Tax charged has actually been paid to the government by the supplier.
  • The relevant return has been filed.
  • Payment to the supplier is made within 180 days of the invoice date, failing which the credit must be reversed with interest.

    • GSTR-2B and the Invoice Management System (IMS)

    GSTR-2B is a static, auto-generated monthly statement showing eligible and ineligible ITC based on suppliers' filings — it is the primary reference for what can be claimed in GSTR-3B. The Invoice Management System lets a recipient accept, reject, or keep pending each inward invoice reflected from a supplier's GSTR-1/1A/IFF, before it flows into GSTR-2B and, from there, into GSTR-3B.

    Blocked credits — Section 17(5)

  • Motor vehicles for transport of persons with seating capacity up to 13 (unless used for further supply, passenger transport, or driving training).
  • Food and beverages, outdoor catering, health services, cosmetic and plastic surgery — unless used to make a further outward taxable supply of the same category.
  • Membership of a club, health or fitness centre.
  • Travel benefits extended to employees on vacation, such as leave or home travel concession.
  • Works contract services for construction of an immovable property (other than plant and machinery), except where it is an input service for further supply of works contract service.
  • Goods or services used for personal consumption.
  • Tax paid under the composition scheme, or tax paid due to fraud, suppression, or wilful misstatement (Sections 74, 129, 130).

    • Hard-locking of GSTR-3B liability

    From the July 2025 tax period (filed August 2025) onward, sales figures auto-populated into GSTR-3B Tables 3.1 and 3.2 from GSTR-1/1A/IFF have become non-editable. Corrections must be made through GSTR-1A for the same period, or through a subsequent GSTR-1/IFF filing — not by manually overriding the number inside GSTR-3B itself.

    What composition dealers cannot do

  • Cannot claim Input Tax Credit on purchases.
  • Cannot collect tax separately from customers on the invoice.
  • Cannot make inter-state outward supplies (with limited exceptions for services).
  • Cannot supply goods through an e-commerce operator required to collect TCS.

    • Composition taxpayers file CMP-08 quarterly and GSTR-4 annually — they are outside the regular GSTR-1/GSTR-3B cycle entirely.

    e-Invoicing

    Real-time B2B invoice authentication through the Invoice Registration Portal (IRP)

    Mandatory thresholdAATO above ₹5 crore, in force since 1 August 2023
    30-day IRP reporting windowMandatory for AATO of ₹10 crore or more (since April 2025)

    Aggregate Annual Turnover (AATO) is measured PAN-wide, across every GSTIN under that PAN, and checked against every financial year since 2017–18. Once a business crosses the applicable threshold in any year, e-invoicing becomes mandatory permanently — it does not lapse even if turnover later drops.

    What's covered

  • B2B invoices, B2G invoices, exports, and credit/debit notes tied to those supplies.
  • Applies whether the entity's turnover is measured by taxable, exempt, or export supply value combined.

    • What's exempt regardless of turnover

  • Banks, NBFCs and insurers.
  • Goods Transport Agencies and passenger transport operators.
  • Multiplex cinemas.
  • Pure B2C transactions — e-invoicing applies to B2B supplies only.

    • Non-compliance carries a penalty of ₹10,000 per invoice, and — just as importantly — the buyer cannot claim ITC on a purchase invoice that was never issued through the IRP.

    e-Way Bill

    Rule 138, CGST Rules, 2017 — required for movement of goods

    Standard thresholdConsignment value exceeding ₹50,000

    An e-way bill is required for the movement of goods worth more than ₹50,000, whether inter-state or intra-state, with the threshold and certain document requirements varying somewhat by state for intra-state movement. It is generated on the e-way bill portal and is distinct from — but often generated alongside — the e-invoice IRN.

    Reverse Charge Mechanism (RCM)

    Section 9(3) & 9(4), CGST Act — the recipient pays the tax instead of the supplier

    Ordinarily the supplier collects GST from the recipient and remits it. Under reverse charge, that flips: the recipient pays GST directly to the government, on specified goods and services, or when receiving supplies from an unregistered person in notified cases. RCM liability must be discharged in cash — it cannot be paid using accumulated Input Tax Credit — though once paid, the same amount can generally be claimed back as ITC in the same return, if otherwise eligible.

    Notified goods under RCM — Section 9(3)

  • Cashew nuts (not shelled or peeled), from an agriculturist.
  • Bidi wrapper leaves (tendu) and tobacco leaves, from an agriculturist.
  • Raw cotton, from an agriculturist.
  • Silk yarn, from any person manufacturing it from raw silk or silk worm cocoons.
  • Supply of lottery, by state government/authorised person to a lottery distributor or selling agent.
  • Used vehicles, seized/confiscated goods, old and used goods, waste and scrap, supplied by government to a registered person.

    • Notified services under RCM — Section 9(3)

  • Goods Transport Agency (GTA) services, where the GTA has not opted to pay under forward charge.
  • Legal services from an advocate or firm of advocates, to a business entity.
  • Services of an arbitral tribunal, to a business entity.
  • Sponsorship services, to any body corporate or partnership firm.
  • Services by the Central/State Government or local authority, excluding specifically excluded categories (e.g. renting of immovable property to an unregistered person, speed post, services by Department of Posts other than express parcel).
  • Services of a director to the company or body corporate.
  • Services of an insurance agent, to the insurance business.
  • Services of a recovery agent, to a bank, NBFC or financial institution.
  • Copyright transfer/permission by an author, music composer, artist, or similar, to a publisher, music company, producer.
  • Renting of a motor vehicle, by a supplier paying tax at 5%, to a body corporate.
  • Services by a business facilitator, to a banking company.
  • Security services (other than by a body corporate), to a registered person.
  • Renting of residential dwelling, to a registered person (since 18 July 2022 — irrespective of use).
  • Import of services, from a supplier located outside India.

    • Section 9(4) — supplies from unregistered persons

    RCM on inward supplies from an unregistered supplier to a registered recipient applies only to specifically notified classes of registered persons and supplies (for example, promoters receiving construction services or cement from unregistered suppliers under the real-estate scheme) — it is not a blanket rule covering every unregistered-to-registered transaction, unlike the position originally proposed when GST launched.

    Compliance obligations under RCM

  • The recipient must issue a self-invoice where the supplier is unregistered, and a payment voucher at the time of payment.
  • RCM liability is reported in Table 3.1(d) of GSTR-3B, and the corresponding ITC claimed in Table 4.
  • Time of supply for RCM is the earliest of: the date of payment, or 30 days (60 for goods) from the date of the supplier's invoice.

    • Offenses, penalties & prosecution

    Sections 122, 129, 130 and 132, CGST Act — beyond ordinary late filing

    Section 122 — specified offenses

    The Act lists 21 specific offenses, including supplying goods or services without an invoice, issuing an invoice without an actual supply, collecting tax but failing to remit it within 3 months, taking or utilising ITC without actual receipt of goods or services, fraudulently obtaining a refund, and failing to maintain required accounts and records.

    CategoryPenalty
    Major offenses (tax evasion, fraud, fake ITC, etc.)₹10,000, or the amount of tax evaded/ITC wrongly availed, whichever is higher
    Other contraventions with no specific penalty prescribedUp to ₹25,000

    Section 129 — detention & seizure of goods in transit

    Goods (and the vehicle carrying them) moving without proper documentation — such as a missing e-way bill or invoice — can be detained or seized.

  • Owner comes forward: tax payable, plus a penalty equal to 100% of the tax (for taxable goods) or a specified percentage of the goods' value (for exempt goods).
  • Owner does not come forward: penalty equal to 50% of the value of goods (less tax paid), or the applicable exempt-goods rate.
  • Goods and conveyance are released on payment of the applicable tax and penalty, or on furnishing a bond with security.

    • Section 130 — confiscation

    Goods or a conveyance used to evade tax through specified contraventions — such as supply or receipt made with intent to evade tax — can be confiscated outright. A fine is imposed in lieu of confiscation, generally referenced to the market value of the goods, less any tax paid.

    Section 132 — prosecution

    Prosecution applies where tax evasion, fraudulent ITC claims, or fraudulent refunds cross specified monetary thresholds — most offenses now require evasion above ₹2 crore before prosecution can be initiated, following the Finance Act, 2023 amendment. Imprisonment ranges from 6 months up to 5 years depending on the amount evaded, with offenses above ₹5 crore treated as cognizable and non-bailable.

    Most GST offenses can be compounded — settled by paying a compounding amount instead of facing prosecution — except in specified serious or repeat-offense circumstances.

    The registration process

    Form GST REG-01 — end to end

    Part A — identity verification

    PAN, mobile number and email are validated by OTP; on success, a Temporary Reference Number (TRN) is generated.

    Part B — business details

    Using the TRN, complete business details, principal and additional places of business, bank account, and authorised signatory details, and upload the supporting documents.

    Application Reference Number (ARN)

    On submission, an ARN is generated, which can be used to track the application's status.

    Aadhaar authentication / biometric verification

    For applicants flagged as risky by the system, biometric Aadhaar authentication and document verification at a GST Seva Kendra is now required before approval.

    Officer review

    A proper officer reviews the application — typically within 7 working days for standard applications, longer where physical verification or Aadhaar authentication is triggered — and may raise a query in Form REG-03.

    Certificate of registration

    On approval, the GSTIN is allotted and the Certificate of Registration is issued in Form REG-06.

    Documents typically required

  • PAN card of the business and the authorised signatory; Aadhaar of the proprietor/partners/directors.
  • Passport-size photograph of the proprietor/partners/directors/authorised signatory.
  • Proof of business constitution — partnership deed, Certificate of Incorporation, or LLP agreement, as applicable.
  • Proof of principal place of business — electricity bill, property tax receipt, rent/lease agreement with a No-Objection Certificate, as applicable.
  • Bank account proof — cancelled cheque, bank statement, or the first page of a passbook.
  • Digital signature (mandatory for companies and LLPs; optional, with e-sign/EVC alternatives, for other entity types).
  • Letter of authorisation or Board resolution appointing the authorised signatory.

    • Refunds under GST

    Section 54, CGST Act — Form RFD-01

    Common refund scenarios

  • Export of goods or services — with payment of IGST (claim refund of the IGST paid), or without payment under a Letter of Undertaking (claim refund of accumulated ITC instead).
  • Inverted duty structure — where the tax rate on inputs is higher than on the output supply, causing ITC to accumulate.
  • Excess balance in the electronic cash ledger.
  • Deemed exports (e.g. supplies to EOUs, under specified schemes).
  • Refund arising from a finalised provisional assessment, or from an appellate or court order.
  • Excess payment of tax due to a mistake or inadvertence.

    • Process & timelines

    StepTimeline
    ApplicationForm RFD-01 on the GST portal, within 2 years of the "relevant date" for that category of refund
    Provisional refund (zero-rated supplies only)90% released within 7 days of acknowledgment, subject to risk-based checks
    Final orderWithin 60 days of a complete application, under Section 54(7)
    Interest on delayed refund6% p.a. if not paid within 60 days; 9% p.a. where the refund arises from an appellate or court order and isn't paid within 60 days of that order

    A minimum refund amount of ₹1,000 applies — claims below this threshold are not processed. Refund claims are also subject to the unjust enrichment principle: the applicant must show the tax burden wasn't passed on to another person, except in specified categories like exports and inverted duty structure.

    Where GST shows up in the Income Tax Act

    GST and income tax are separate laws, but they intersect at several specific points

    A GST-registered business doesn't just file GST returns in isolation — several provisions of the Income Tax Act, 1961 directly reference how GST is treated in computing taxable income, and GST filings are increasingly cross-checked against income tax filings for consistency.

    Section 43B(a)
    Deduction only on actual payment

    Any GST liability is deductible while computing business income only in the year it is actually paid — not merely accrued or provided for. A payment made after the financial year-end but before the due date for filing the return of income under Section 139(1) is still treated as paid within that year, which matters because March's GST liability is typically paid in April, after the financial year has closed.

    Section 145A
    Inclusive method of valuation

    Purchases, sales, and closing inventory must be valued inclusive of any tax, duty, cess or fee actually incurred (i.e., GST included), for the purpose of computing income under "Profits and gains of business or profession." Applied consistently across purchases, sales and both opening and closing stock, the net taxable profit works out the same as under the exclusive method — but the presentation and cross-checks change.

    Section 37(1)
    GST as a business expense

    GST paid on business purchases is an allowable deduction as ordinary business expenditure. Where GST paid on an eligible purchase could have been claimed as Input Tax Credit but wasn't, the un-claimed GST becomes part of the cost of the goods or service instead. Penalties or fines paid under GST law for infraction of law are not deductible, under the Explanation to Section 37(1) — though ordinary interest for delayed payment is generally treated as compensatory, not penal, and remains deductible.

    Sections 194Q & 206C(1H)
    Income-tax TDS/TCS exclude the GST component

    Where TDS under Section 194Q (on purchase of goods) or TCS under Section 206C(1H) (on sale of goods) applies, the GST component is excluded from the value on which tax is deducted or collected, provided GST is separately indicated on the invoice — per CBDT clarification. This income-tax TDS/TCS is entirely distinct from GST's own TDS (Section 51, CGST Act) and TCS (Section 52, CGST Act) mechanisms, which operate independently under GST law.

    Tax Audit — Form 3CD
    GST turnover reconciliation

    Clause 44 of the tax audit report (Form 3CD) calls for a break-up of total expenditure between GST-registered and unregistered entities. Auditors and assessing officers also routinely reconcile the turnover reported in GSTR-1/GSTR-9 against the turnover reported in the income tax return and audited financial statements — a mismatch is a common trigger for further scrutiny.

    Sections 44AD / 44ADA
    Presumptive taxation turnover

    For computing turnover under the presumptive taxation schemes, turnover is generally taken exclusive of GST — consistent with how "turnover" is understood under accounting standards, since GST collected is not part of the business's own income.

    AIS / Project Insight
    Cross-verification with GST data

    GST return data (GSTR-1, GSTR-3B, GSTR-9 turnover figures) feeds into the Income Tax Department's data-analytics and risk-assessment systems, and can surface in a taxpayer's Annual Information Statement. Significant, unexplained gaps between GST-reported turnover and income-tax-reported turnover are a well-established scrutiny trigger.

    None of these provisions make GST itself an income-tax matter — GST compliance and income-tax compliance remain governed by separate Acts, separate returns, and separate authorities. What they create is a set of numbers — turnover, expense, and payment timing — that need to tie out consistently across both filings.
